### Q4. pass@k by hand

With three attempts and one correct answer, there are two wrong answers. `pass_at_k()` in [harness/metrics.py](https://github.com/ga424/cs690_assignment1/blob/0fb34cbc159d1438802f2d1cae2b964b4b86238f/cs690-a1-controlled-eval/harness/metrics.py) uses `1 - C(n-c,k)/C(n,k)`. Here, `C(a,b)` counts the ways to choose b distinct items from a items.

- **pass@1:** `1 - C(2,1)/C(3,1) = 1 - 2/3 = 1/3`.
- **pass@2:** `1 - C(2,2)/C(3,2) = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3`.

The function returned:

```text
pass_at_k(3, 1, 1) = 0.33333333333333337
pass_at_k(3, 1, 2) = 0.6666666666666667
```

The shortcut gives `1 - (1 - 1/3)**2 = 5/9`, or `0.5555555555555555` in Python. The difference comes from whether an attempt can be picked twice. The shortcut allows that. The correct calculation chooses two distinct attempts. Of the three possible pairs, two include the correct answer, so pass@2 is two-thirds.

### Q5. Why whole problems are redrawn

`bootstrap_task_ci()` in [harness/metrics.py](https://github.com/ga424/cs690_assignment1/blob/0fb34cbc159d1438802f2d1cae2b964b4b86238f/cs690-a1-controlled-eval/harness/metrics.py) starts by checking its inputs, sorting the task IDs, and calculating each task's score. Using seed 690, it randomly builds another set of 20 tasks from the recorded results, allowing a task to appear more than once. It averages those scores and repeats this 5,000 times. It then sorts the averages and uses `_percentile()` to find the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

The idea is to see how the result changes when the mix of problems changes. Attempts on the same problem share its difficulty, so drawing attempts separately understates that uncertainty. In [tests/test_metrics.py](https://github.com/ga424/cs690_assignment1/blob/0fb34cbc159d1438802f2d1cae2b964b4b86238f/cs690-a1-controlled-eval/tests/test_metrics.py), `test_task_bootstrap_resamples_tasks_not_candidate_rows()` checks one task that always passes and one that always fails, with three attempts each. Drawing either task twice produces scores of 1 or 0, and the test requires an interval of [0, 1]. Drawing individual attempts instead gives a narrower range.

## Part 5. Reading a published score

**Benchmark chosen: HumanEval**

### 1. What does it measure?

HumanEval measures whether a model can write a small Python function from its name, inputs, and a description of what it should do. Chen et al. (2021) introduced 164 handwritten problems and checked the answers with unit tests. The code can differ from the reference solution and still pass. The pass@k score estimates how often at least one of k attempts passes the tests, averaged across the problems. That is a useful measure of success on these particular tasks. [Chen et al. (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.03374v2#S2).

### 2. What does it not measure that a software project may depend on?

For a software project, I would want to know more than whether a model can finish one function. Can it understand an unfamiliar codebase, change the right files, and avoid breaking existing features? Based on its task definition, HumanEval does not establish those abilities. It also does not guarantee readable, secure, or maintainable code. Even passing the tests can miss mistakes. Liu et al. (2023) added tests through HumanEval+ and found incorrect answers that the original tests had accepted. [Liu et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01210).

### 3. How can a reported score rise without the underlying model becoming better?

A score can rise simply by allowing more attempts and reporting pass@10 instead of pass@1. The model gets more chances to produce a passing answer without any change to its training. The original paper also shows that changing temperature affects performance. Using a weaker test suite can make answers look more successful, as the HumanEval+ study demonstrates. Before comparing two published scores, I would check how many attempts were allowed, the generation settings, and which tests decided whether an answer passed. [Chen et al. (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.03374v2#S3.S3), [Liu et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01210).

### 4. Could the model have seen the answers already?

Yes, and there is evidence beyond the possibility. Riddell, Ni, and Cohan (2024) measured overlap between HumanEval solutions and public training datasets. They also found that the models they studied performed better on problems with similar solutions in their training data. This supports taking contamination seriously. However, it does not tell me whether Luna or Terra saw a particular answer, or how much that affected their scores. I would need evidence about their training data to make that claim. [Riddell et al. (2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.761/).

A published HumanEval score is not interchangeable with my `CS690-Eval20` result because the problems, tests, prompts, and evaluation settings differ.
